Distribution & Shipping

Perhaps this is the most overlooked stage: we care that the product arrives, but rarely think about everything that has to happen before it does.

Movement Through the Supply Chain

Distribution is easy to overlook because we mostly notice one thing: the product arrived. But getting a finished lip balm from production to a warehouse, retailer, or customer is another major part of its environmental story.

A sustainability analysis citing research from MIT Sloan Management Review and The Boston Consulting Group reports that a company’s supply chain can contain substantially more greenhouse gas emissions than its internal operations, making transport logistics a critical area for eco-conscious brands to address.

Once packaged, the balm moves to a storage facility before reaching retailers. Because natural lip balms are formulated without synthetic stabilizers, they can soften in high temperatures. Temperature-controlled fulfillment guidance for cosmetics explains why heat, moisture, and temperature changes can affect product integrity, while warehouse guidance comparing temperature-controlled and ambient storage shows why climate-controlled facilities require more specialized infrastructure and energy.

Freight density matters too. Utilizing lighter, space-optimized fibre-based and aluminium-based containers reduces the total weight and volume that needs to be moved.

B2B: Bulk Retail

In a business-to-business (B2B) route, larger quantities are transported en masse to retailer distribution centres, where they are later placed on retail shelves.

D2C: E-Commerce

In a direct-to-consumer (D2C) route, individual orders are selected at a fulfillment centre and packaged for direct residential delivery. Research on packaging as a last-mile logistics decision found that consumers tend to prioritise practical sustainability and functional protection over purely experiential packaging.

Environmental Considerations

Transportation choices dictate a brand’s carbon emissions, while packaging choices affect total material waste. Total freight emissions depend directly on factors like the transport method (e.g. ground vs. air) and structural freight density.

The Distribution Paradox
A balm that is sensitive to heat may need temperature-controlled transport, but active cooling systems require massive inputs of electricity. Industry data regarding temp-controlled vs. ambient warehouses confirms that cooled storage consumes substantially more energy than ambient facilities. The solution involves balancing necessary product protection with transport efficiency.

Another concern is secondary packaging. Single-use stretch wrap and over-packaging can add unnecessary material and take up space, particularly in e-commerce fulfillment. A greener supply chain must constantly evaluate what truly protects the product—and what simply becomes immediate landfill waste.

What Makes This Stage More Sustainable?

The project groups sustainable distribution around five core ideas: Moving toward zero-emission transit chains; Avoiding unnecessary plastic secondary packaging; Maximising freight density and payload efficiency; Delivering according to accurate market demand; Supporting closed-loop recycling or refill systems.

Together, these ideas move the product journey toward a more circular system where fewer resources are lost along the way.
